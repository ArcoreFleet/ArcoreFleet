A plain-language blueprint

Build Your Own Cloud

How a small business can run its own apps and websites safely — using a spare computer, one cheap cloud server, and mostly free tools — without hiring an IT department or paying for expensive hosting.

The big picture

Most small businesses either pay a hosting company a recurring fee for every website or tool they run, or they let apps live on whatever computer happens to be handy, with no real plan for security. There's a third option: connect a computer you already own to a free-tier cloud server, using free tools originally built for large tech companies, and get something more secure and more reliable than most paid small-business hosting — for the cost of a domain name.

Here's the shape of it, in order of how a visitor's request actually travels:

Nothing about your server is ever exposed directly to the internet. Nobody can "find" it and try to break in, because there is no door for them to find — only a one-way tunnel your own server opens outward, the same way your phone can receive text messages without anyone needing to dial a number straight into it.

Think of it like a storefront with a locked back office. Customers walk in the front door (your website address), a receptionist checks they're allowed where they're going (Cloudflare), and are shown to the right room (the traffic director). Staff reach the back office — the stuff customers never see — through a private hallway that doesn't connect to the street at all (your private network).

What you'll need

  • A domain name you own — e.g. yourbusiness.com — typically $10-20 a year from any registrar
  • One computer you already own that can stay turned on (an old office PC works fine)
  • One free-tier cloud server — Oracle Cloud's "Always Free" tier gives you a real server, for real, forever, at $0/month
  • A free Cloudflare account
  • A free Tailscale account
  • Comfort following written technical instructions, or a freelancer who is (see Getting Help)

The steps

Each step below builds on the last. None of them require you to open anything on your office router or buy a traditional web hosting plan.

1

Point your domain at Cloudflare

$015 min, mostly clicking

A domain name is just a sign-up sheet that tells the internet which computer to send visitors to. Right now that sheet lives with whoever you bought the domain from. You move it to Cloudflare — a company that provides the next few steps for free — by changing two settings at your registrar.

It's the same as updating your business's forwarding address with the post office. The business doesn't move. Mail just gets redirected.

Don't lose your email or website

If you already have a website or business email, have whoever manages your domain double-check every existing setting gets copied over before you flip the switch. This is the one step worth paying a professional an hour for if you're not confident — undoing a mistake here can mean a day of lost email.

2

Connect your computers with a private network

$010 min per computer

Install Tailscale on your office computer and on your cloud server. It quietly connects them into their own private network — as if you ran a cable directly between your office and the cloud, except the "cable" is encrypted and works from anywhere.

This is a private hallway, not a public road. Only computers you've personally approved can ever walk down it. The public internet can't see it exists.
3

Install a traffic director

$020 min

One piece of software, called a reverse proxy, sits in front of all your apps and sends each visitor to the right one based on the address they typed — invoices.yourbusiness.com goes to your invoicing app, shop.yourbusiness.com goes to your storefront. You run one of these, not one per app.

It's a receptionist standing at the one door everyone walks through, pointing each visitor to the right office.
4

Open a one-way tunnel to the outside world

$015 min

Normally, putting something on the internet means opening a "door" on your server that anyone can knock on — which is exactly what attackers scan the internet looking for, all day, every day. Instead, your server makes an outbound-only connection to Cloudflare and keeps it open. The public internet is handed to Cloudflare, who relays it down that tunnel. Your server never accepts an incoming connection from a stranger.

Think of a mail slot versus a front door. A mail slot only ever pushes things one way through a small, controlled opening. There's no doorknob on the outside for anyone to try.
5

Require a login — using accounts your team already has

$0 for small teams30-45 min

For anything that shouldn't be fully public — an internal tool, a client portal, admin screens — add a rule that says "only people who sign in with an approved Google or Microsoft account get through." Nobody needs a new password; they use the same Google or Microsoft 365 login they already have. You simply give Cloudflare a list of approved emails (or approve an entire company email domain at once).

It's a guest list at the door, checked against an ID your visitors already carry, instead of a new key you'd have to hand out and track.
6

Get a dashboard, so you're not stuck typing commands

$015 min

Install Portainer, a web dashboard for seeing and managing everything running on your server(s) with clicks instead of typed commands. It's genuinely useful day-to-day — and genuinely powerful, which is exactly why it's kept reachable only over the private network from Step 2, never from the public internet. A tool with this much control over your systems should never sit behind nothing but a login page on the open web.

The one mistake to avoid

It's tempting to route a dashboard like this through Step 4's public tunnel for convenience. Don't. Keep every admin tool reachable only through your private network (Step 2). If you ever do want it reachable from outside your office, put it behind the login gate from Step 5 first — never leave it open on its own.

What it costs

Compared to a managed VPN appliance, a traditional web host, and separate login/security software, the total ongoing cost of this setup is close to nothing — most of it is free-tier cloud and open-source software that happens to be excellent.

Staying secure

What makes this safer than it sounds

No port is ever opened to the public internet on any of your own machines. Every public request is checked by Cloudflare first. Admin tools are reachable only from your own private network. Your public website and business email are left completely untouched by any of this — they keep working exactly as they do today.

Three habits matter more than any specific tool:

  • Never expose an admin tool (dashboards, databases, management panels) to the public internet "for convenience" — reach it over your private network instead
  • Keep a written, dated list of every password, access key, and login you set up — store it somewhere only your team can reach, never in an email or shared chat
  • Review who has access every few months, and remove anyone who's left the business the same day they leave

Looking ahead

Everything above gets a business safely online. As you grow, three more pieces are worth adding, in this order:

  • A shared database — one well-backed-up place for all your apps to store information, instead of each app keeping its own
  • A repeatable template — so adding your 2nd, 10th, or 50th internal app takes an afternoon, not a redesign
  • Automatic deployment — so updating an app is "save and walk away" instead of a manual server visit

None of these are urgent for a business running a handful of tools. They start paying off once you're running many.

Glossary

Domain name
The address people type, like yourbusiness.com. You rent it yearly; you never fully "own" it.
DNS
The internet's phone book — it turns a domain name into the actual location of a server.
Cloud server
A computer you rent by the month (or, here, for free) that lives in a data center instead of your office.
Private network / mesh
A network only your own approved devices can join, regardless of where in the world each one physically is.
Reverse proxy
Software that receives every visitor and decides which app should actually handle them.
Tunnel
An outbound-only connection that lets your server be reached without ever accepting an unsolicited inbound connection.
SSO (single sign-on)
Letting people log in with an account they already have (Google, Microsoft) instead of creating yet another password.
Container
A small, self-contained package that an app runs inside — like a shipping container, the same box works on any truck, ship, or server.
Cluster
Several servers working together and sharing the load, managed as if they were one.

Getting help

Every step above can be done by a motivated non-technical owner willing to follow instructions carefully, or handed to a freelance systems administrator for a few hours of paid work — this is a small, well-defined project, not an ongoing contract. If you hire someone, ask them specifically to:

  • Keep every admin tool off the public internet, reachable only via the private network
  • Write down every account, login, and setting they create, in a document they hand you — not just in their own head
  • Explain, in plain language, what each piece does — if they can't, that's worth a second opinion
Written as a general-purpose reference. Your own setup may need small adjustments — talk to whoever's helping you implement it about your specific apps, team size, and existing website or email provider before changing any live settings.
